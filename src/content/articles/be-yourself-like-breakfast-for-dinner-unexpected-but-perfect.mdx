A lesson in job hunting, leadership, and why being yourself usually works better than trying to perform the perfect version of yourself.

You ever have breakfast for dinner?

At first, it feels wrong. Pancakes at 7 p.m.? Eggs and bacon when the sun is setting? But then, you take a bite, and it just makes sense. It’s satisfying, it’s familiar, and it works because it breaks the norm.

That’s exactly what it’s like when you stop trying to be who you think people want you to be and just show up as yourself.

## The performance trap

At the start of The Breakfast Club, you’ve got five kids from different walks of life, each playing into some version of what they think they’re supposed to be. The brain, the athlete, the basket case, the princess, the criminal. But by the end of the movie, those labels don’t matter. The moment they drop the act and just are themselves is when they actually connect.

That’s what happens when you stop trying to fit into some job description like it’s a box you have to squeeze into. The truth is, the people who click with you, who get you, aren’t looking for someone playing a role. They’re looking for someone real. Someone who’s honest about what they bring to the table. Someone they can trust.

## Why we try to be something else

At some point, we all feel the need to perform. Whether it’s job hunting, giving a presentation, selling something, or just walking into a room full of strangers, there’s a natural tendency to put on a version of ourselves that we think the situation demands. It’s still us, but slightly altered, tweaked, polished, and sometimes completely reconstructed.

When I was job hunting, I fell into this trap. I wanted to be the person I thought hiring managers were looking for. I tried to present myself as hyper-polished, ultra-articulate, and meticulously detail-oriented, the perfect candidate. But in reality? I was stiff, uncomfortable, and at times, probably giving off the exact opposite impression of what I was hoping for.

## The problem with playing a role

We assume that just being ourselves isn’t enough. If a job description asks for a hyper-detailed, process-driven, documentation-obsessed person, we suddenly convince ourselves that our organized but sometimes intuitive nature isn’t good enough, so we tweak our narrative. We sell an image of ourselves that isn’t false, but maybe isn’t fully accurate either.

But here’s the problem. If you “sell” something that doesn’t reflect who you actually are, one of two things happens:

- You get the job or opportunity and now have to maintain the act.
- People see through it anyway.

> The people who click with you are not looking for someone playing a role. They are looking for someone real.

## The moment everything changed for me

Earlier this year, I took on a consulting role. From day one, I had nothing to lose. I was being paid to be critical, to ask why, to dig into what worked and what didn’t. There was no need to put on a show because my job was to be myself. Meanwhile, I was also interviewing for another job in an industry I was comfortable with, and because I knew I could do it, I didn’t overthink how I presented myself; I just was.

For months before that, I had been in job-search survival mode. I tweaked my answers to sound how I thought companies wanted to hear them, sent out applications into the void, and faced nothing but silence or rejection. But when I stopped trying so hard to mold myself into something else, everything shifted. Suddenly, I had two offers. A new kind of stress, because now I had to choose, but a good problem to have. The difference? I wasn’t selling a version of myself anymore. I was just selling me.

## The real benefit of being yourself

The biggest thing I realized? When I was myself, I found people like me.

I clicked with my future manager because we had the same mindset and approach. I connected with my new team because they valued the things I naturally brought to the table. And in a work environment, that’s everything.

Think about it, who do you work best with? The people you can actually relate to. The ones who get your thought process, who see your strengths, and who don’t expect you to be someone you’re not. Being yourself isn’t just about landing the job, it’s about making sure you’re in a place where you can thrive.

And if you want to be a leader, the same rule applies. People follow those they trust, not those putting on a performance. The best teams aren’t built on people pretending to be something they’re not, they’re built on people who genuinely connect with one another.

So yeah, maybe you’re not “the most detail-oriented, data-driven, process-following person to ever live.” Maybe you are a little more gut-driven, a little scrappy, a little unconventional. But somewhere out there, there’s a team that needs exactly that.

And just like breakfast for dinner, it doesn’t follow the rules, but somehow, it’s exactly right.
